Ultraportable Laptops Compared: MacBook Air M3, Dell XPS 13 Plus, and ThinkPad X1 Nano Gen 4 – Real-World Performance and Value

2026-06-13 · Laptops · Tags: techgadgetreviews, laptops, Ultraportable

In the ever-evolving world of laptops, the ultraportable category has become the battleground for the best blend of power, portability, and price. For 2025, three contenders stand out: Apple's MacBook Air M3, Dell's XPS 13 Plus, and Lenovo's ThinkPad X1 Nano Gen 4. Each promises to be the ultimate travel companion, but they take radically different approaches to design, performance, and user experience. As a tech reviewer who spends more hours than I'd like to admit testing these machines, I've put all three through rigorous real-world scenarios—from 4K video editing to marathon coding sessions and everything in between. This isn't just a spec sheet comparison; it's a deep dive into how these laptops actually perform when you're on the go, under deadline, and juggling a dozen tabs. Let's find out which one deserves your hard-earned money.

The Contenders: Specs That Matter

Before we get into the nitty-gritty, let's establish the baseline. The MacBook Air M3 (13-inch) comes with Apple's latest 3nm chip, offering an 8-core CPU and up to 10-core GPU, with options for 8GB to 24GB of unified memory. The Dell XPS 13 Plus is powered by Intel's 13th-gen Raptor Lake processors (up to i7-1370P) with Iris Xe graphics, and it can be configured with up to 32GB of LPDDR5 RAM. The ThinkPad X1 Nano Gen 4, meanwhile, uses Intel's 13th-gen P-series chips (up to i7-1370P) as well, but with a focus on extreme lightness—it tips the scales at just 2.12 pounds. All three have gorgeous displays: the MacBook Air's Liquid Retina (2560x1664), the XPS 13 Plus's OLED touch (3456x2160), and the X1 Nano's 2K (2160x1350) IPS panel. But specs only tell half the story. The real question is how these components translate into daily usability.

Real-World Performance: Benchmarks and Daily Use

I ran a suite of benchmarks and, more importantly, used each laptop as my primary driver for a week. In Geekbench 6, the MacBook Air M3 scored around 3,100 single-core and 12,000 multi-core, which is impressive but not drastically ahead of the Intel competitors. However, the M3's efficiency is where it shines. When rendering a 4K video in DaVinci Resolve, the MacBook Air completed the task in 4 minutes 32 seconds, while the XPS 13 Plus took 6 minutes 10 seconds, and the X1 Nano lagged at 7 minutes 45 seconds. The difference is stark, especially considering the MacBook Air is fanless—it never throttled, while both Intel laptops spun up their fans and reduced clock speeds after prolonged load. For everyday tasks like web browsing, document editing, and even light photo editing, all three are snappy. But if you're a content creator or a developer who compiles code, the M3's sustained performance is a game-changer. The XPS 13 Plus, with its higher TDP, can match the M3 in short bursts, but it quickly hits thermal limits. The X1 Nano, despite its size, holds its own for office work but struggles with anything graphics-intensive.

Battery Life and Portability: The Ultraportable Test

Battery life is the ultimate metric for an ultraportable. I looped a 1080p video at 50% brightness with Wi-Fi on and earbuds connected. The MacBook Air M3 lasted an astonishing 18 hours and 22 minutes—the best I've ever recorded. The XPS 13 Plus, with its OLED display, managed 9 hours 45 minutes, which is decent but not stellar. The X1 Nano, despite its small 49.6Wh battery, squeezed out 11 hours 12 minutes, thanks to its efficient IPS panel and Intel's Evo platform. In real-world mixed usage, the MacBook Air easily gets you through two full workdays without a charge, while the others will need a midday top-up. Portability is another story: the X1 Nano is the clear winner at 2.12 pounds, followed by the MacBook Air at 2.7 pounds, and the XPS 13 Plus at 2.8 pounds. The X1 Nano's carbon fiber chassis makes it feel almost ethereal, but it also means a slightly flexier keyboard deck. The MacBook Air's aluminum unibody is rock solid, and the XPS 13 Plus's glass palm rest is premium but prone to fingerprints.

Keyboard, Trackpad, and Build Quality: The Feel Factor

You'll be typing on these machines for hours, so the input experience matters. The ThinkPad X1 Nano inherits Lenovo's legendary keyboard—deep travel, tactile feedback, and a layout that's pure muscle memory for many. It's the best of the three, hands down. The MacBook Air's Magic Keyboard is excellent too, with a satisfying scissor mechanism, but it's slightly shallower than the ThinkPad's. The XPS 13 Plus, however, is a controversial departure: it features a zero-lattice keyboard with a hidden haptic trackpad that's integrated into the palm rest. The keys are wide and flat, and while I adapted after a few days, the lack of physical separation can lead to typos, especially for touch typists. The haptic trackpad is responsive, but it doesn't offer the same tactile click as the MacBook's Force Touch or the ThinkPad's physical buttons. Build quality is uniformly high, but the XPS 13 Plus's glass top and soft-touch finish feel more fragile than the others. The MacBook Air's aluminum is the most durable, and the X1 Nano's carbon fiber is light but can show wear over time.

Value for Money: What You Get for Your Dollar

Pricing is where things get interesting. The base MacBook Air M3 starts at $1,099 with 8GB RAM and 256GB SSD—a configuration that's often criticized for being too little, but Apple's unified memory architecture makes it more usable than you'd think. The Dell XPS 13 Plus starts at $1,299 for an i5, 16GB RAM, and 512GB SSD, but you'll want the OLED upgrade, which pushes it to $1,499. The ThinkPad X1 Nano Gen 4 starts at $1,399 for an i5, 16GB RAM, and 512GB SSD, but it frequently goes on sale for under $1,200. When you compare apples to apples—say, 16GB RAM and 512GB storage—the MacBook Air costs $1,399, the XPS 13 Plus $1,499, and the X1 Nano $1,399 (at sale price). The MacBook Air offers the best performance per dollar, especially if you factor in the longer battery life and the free OS updates. The XPS 13 Plus is the most expensive but gives you a stunning OLED display and a futuristic design. The X1 Nano is the best value for business travelers who prioritize weight and keyboard comfort over raw power. But there's a catch: the MacBook Air's 8GB base model is a trap—you'll want to upgrade to 16GB, which adds $200. Still, even at $1,399, it undercuts the XPS 13 Plus with similar specs.

The Verdict: Which One Should You Buy?

After weeks of testing, the answer depends on your priorities. If you're a creative professional, a student, or anyone who values sustained performance and all-day battery life, the MacBook Air M3 is the clear winner. It's not just the best ultraportable; it's arguably the best laptop you can buy under $1,500. The only downsides are the limited port selection (two Thunderbolt ports) and the lack of a touchscreen, which some users might miss. If you're a Windows loyalist who needs a touchscreen and a stunning OLED display, the Dell XPS 13 Plus is a fantastic choice, but be prepared for shorter battery life and a keyboard that takes getting used to. And if you're a frequent flyer who lives in spreadsheets and emails, the ThinkPad X1 Nano Gen 4 is your soulmate—it's the lightest, has the best keyboard, and offers solid all-day battery life, even if it can't keep up with the M3 in heavy workloads. In the end, there's no single "best" laptop, but there is a best for you. My advice: go to a store, type on all three, and feel the weight. Your fingers and shoulders will thank you.

Conclusion

The ultraportable market has never been more competitive, and the MacBook Air M3, Dell XPS 13 Plus, and ThinkPad X1 Nano Gen 4 each bring something unique to the table. The MacBook Air redefines efficiency and performance, the XPS 13 Plus pushes design boundaries, and the X1 Nano masters the art of lightness. As a reviewer, I'm often asked which one I'd buy with my own money—and that's the MacBook Air M3, without hesitation. But that's because my workflow demands sustained performance and battery life. If your priorities differ, don't be afraid to choose the one that fits your lifestyle. After all, the best laptop is the one you'll actually carry with you every day.
